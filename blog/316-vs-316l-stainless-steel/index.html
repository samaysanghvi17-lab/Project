316 vs 316L Stainless Steel: What Is the Difference?

316 and 316L stainless differ mainly in carbon content. Learn what that means for welding, strength and dual certification when buying flanges and buttweld fittings.

Grades 316 and 316L are the same alloy family, both with about 2–3% molybdenum for resistance to chlorides. The difference that matters when you buy flanges and fittings is the carbon limit.

The carbon difference

316 allows up to 0.08% carbon. 316L allows up to 0.030%. The lower carbon in 316L reduces the risk of carbide precipitation (sensitization) in the heat-affected zone when you weld, which can otherwise lower corrosion resistance at the weld.

What about strength?

The ASTM minimum tensile strengths are 515 MPa (75 ksi) for 316 and 485 MPa (70 ksi) for 316L, and the minimum yield strengths are 205 MPa (30 ksi) and 170 MPa (25 ksi). Actual strength on your certificate is usually above these minimums.

Dual certification

Modern melts often meet both grades at once, so mills certify material as dual-grade 316/316L. This lets you use one stock for both. If your specification requires a single grade, say so when you order.

Which should you specify?

Specify 316L (or dual-certified 316/316L) for welded construction. Specify 316 where the part is not welded or where the higher carbon is acceptable. For very high chloride service, consider duplex or super duplex instead.
